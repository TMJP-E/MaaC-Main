# Introduction

One of the most fundamental scientific fields throughout human history is philosophy, after all, it is the mother of all sciences, hence, a bit of common ground has to be laid first before we can even start to think about mathematics. To align our mindset and ideas with those that gave birth to it, so it feels natural to us.
# Historical Applications

Throughout all of the human species' history, mathematics has been the most important science, it allows us to do many revolutionary actions, like counting and measuring, distribute resources, build shelters, predict events, know seemingly impossible quantities, tax populations and much more, it has seen usage in every possible science, which makes one wonder, what even *is* math? and why was it used so much?

Mathematics is a *formal* science, that is, it describes abstract structures, ideas that can only exist as concepts of the mind, but not only that, being formal means it can help us deduce results using pure reason, in a way that feels natural and sequential, as if our reasoning was correct, as if it could be successfully applied to reality, since reality follows from a cause-effect relation, if we can utilize correct reasoning to propose arguments that can lead to solutions of our desired problem, we are doing science, and if that is made with those abstract structures, it is a formal science.

This *formalism* notion can be viewed as the usage of well defined rules that can lead to a correct cause and effect description of the world around us, although, only being applied to these concepts of the mind, this describes the notion of formality and its applications, since we have purely conceptual objects we are playing with, that we know, with complete certainty, how to use correctly, and the results we get can later be applied to reality.

What we have just described are both views on math, the more specific definition that limits it to only abstract objects and their relations, how we create and use those objects, and the applications that these ideas have, which, throughout history, have helped us do everything from simple counting and relations of fruits to people, all the way to compound interest and investments, building massive structures, a system of taxes, measuring enormous distances and quantities, predicting seasonal and astronomical events, and so much more, but what I have just described are achievements our species made more than millennia of years ago.
# Philosophy in our life

Our species has always looked for reasons with which to explain our environment, from gods and myths to thought and research, it is imperative in human nature to wonder about our existence and it's reason, purpose and limitations, this gave life to two different questions, why do we exist and how to interpret that.

*Epistemology* is the branch of philosophy that questions the nature of knowledge, beliefs and justifications, *Ontology* studies the being, reality and our presence on it, thus, we are now, as we have always been, concerned about how to think, what can we understand and how it can be justified, as well as, what is reality, how do we exist and why are we here, which brings a nice relation between them, **how can we understand the world around us?**

Science has always been part of our development as humanity, and to think, one needs to abstract reality into a higher state of the mind, ***ideas***, which are then applied to our existence, with a basis that allows us to draw correct conclusions and answer our first question. These thoughts themselves rely on the single most important fact one can learn, that is, facts themselves, truth and falsehoods, the simplest and most minuscule piece of information we can perceive is, if something is true, or if it is false, one could wonder if something can be both true or false, and if this concerns reality, could something be and not be?
# Laws of Thought

The following are the *Laws of thought* proposed by many different authors throughout the years, and, to an extent, our mind can easily align with them if we observe reality, but there are cases in which not all hold, although those types of logic are as valid as any other, these three laws prove extremely useful when trying to think, hence:

1. ***The law of identity***: *'Whatever is, is'*. Simple to understand, if something is, if it exists, if it holds a property and if we accept it, then it simply does.
2. ***The law of contradiction***: *'Nothing can both be and not be'*. Since anything needs to be, it can only be a single one, and when thinking in the smallest possible terms, it either is, or it is not.
3. ***The law of excluded middle***: *'Everything must either be, or not be'*. There is no "third" state which describes something, as if it is, it can only be, and, equivalently, it must be, or if it is not, then it must not.

With this, we can easily understand the lowest possible forms of information, ideas, facts, statements, sentences we propose, that, thanks to these laws, may be, or may not be, true or false, which is a mindset oddly similar to reality, since reality either works one way or it doesn't, and something happens or it doesn't.

These three laws can help us model the entire world around us, and perhaps that model can help us abstract fundamental truths about our existence, or build a system that allows one to simplify intricate ideas into simple symbols.

It would be convenient if we had a way of writing all of these statements without repeating ourselves and using a more specific and adequate language, formalizing this notion with the correspondent definitions, rules and results, we can arrive at the next big milestone, Logic.
# Education flaws

If any of the statements and facts presented prior were not known to you, this means the way we have been teaching mathematics at a basic level hasn't changed much, but, as math itself has become more robust, complex and precise, our methods of learning and teaching should also change, the system has failed us, but it's a byproduct of tradition.

Historically speaking, the earliest knowledge of mathematics was passed down with a *pragmatic methodology* for teaching, since it was a science developed with the purpose of aiding our goals as a species, we didn't look further into the definitions or the reasons why something worked, we only cared about if it worked in our reality and how we use it, this mindset carried on for thousands of years, it was the most effective way of teaching, procedures, algorithms and basic forms that would help you solve everyday problems, however, science and technology have advanced greatly, and mathematics doesn't concern itself with only the applications, but the formality of its nature, and yet, we are still teaching in a way that has been outdated for hundreds of years.

This primer ignores such pragmatism, instead, focusing on the core and roots of something so complex, so we can first think, understand and comprehend, and just then, we'll be able to successfully apply, indeed, if we know the rules of the game, we can play it correctly, if we know how our tools work, we can use them to solve the problems each one has been designed for, even those outside of our reality.
# Conclusions

Mathematics has always been important for as long as humans have existed, and yet, despite how much it has changed, the way it's taught resembles methods used millennia ago, we first have to ponder the reason of things, how to reason correctly, and arrive at sound conclusions that make sense with our own ideas and sometimes with the world around us, before learning any procedures or algorithms.

We care much more about answering how something can be and why it can be than what it could be, the inherent truth that this abstract knowledge has just by itself, the way we can shape our thoughts to align with it, and be more correct and efficient, additionally, we must not follow what we have always been taught before, to do before we think, instead, first we think, then we use.

This mindset will help us construct our first, and arguably, the most important concept in all of mathematics, in fact, one that escapes the scope of math itself, a system capable of describing well reasoning, sounding arguments and correct results, helping us develop valid conclusions, this system is Logic.
