# Preface

Mathematics as a Construct (which will be referred as MaaC from now on) is a series of writings that aim to complement the study of math, it is a passion project derived from my need to help others in understanding the beauty that lies behind math. The aim of this writing work is to demonstrate how one could arrive naturally to all the conclusions we are faithfully taught at school, so that math makes sense as if you could construct it yourself.

This series is not intended to fully teach mathematics[^1], nor will it give you a cheat code for your exams, but it is intended to be used alongside your own learning, as it aims to provide the insight that is usually left aside when talking about something as abstract and complicated as math.

Lastly, before starting, a few things need to be disclosed, mathematics is a broad and complex scientific field, filled to the brim with endless knowledge that keeps piling up every day, as such, one cannot expect to know everything, Socrates would advise to remain humble in front of our own progress, for it is we will never truly be sure of our knowledge, and so, I am not an expert, nor a divine source of truth, and if, at any point, the person who is reading this, needs to do deeper research or reread certain topics, it is more than encouraged, mathematics are intricately related, and you'll always be going back and forth between concepts, objects, branches and more. 

Take your time, value your progress, and forget everything you know about math, because we will construct it from nothing, as it should have always been.

You can already start, go ahead into the [[First Chapter - The Foundations]].

# Structure

The recommended structure for reading this book is, at the pace you want to, on the topics you need to, there is no shame in going back and forth, in the end, mathematics is one big entangled web we are about to dive into.
The topics themselves are covered in a format that allows you either read through each section sequentially or look for terms, results and formulas at the end. It is recommended that you first give a full read through a chapter before continuing, since we are going to construct all concepts from prior knowledge, and then, if any questions remain or you need a refresher, you can look for specific concepts later.

Each chapter, unless stated otherwise, constitutes of the following elements:
1. Chapter Index Name
2. A short description of the objective, contents, key takeaways and conclusions one should get from this chapter, as well as any prerequisite knowledge.
3. List of sections, each one with a brief description too.
4. Results Section, as a dedicated space that sums up definitions, concepts, axioms, theorems, objects and such similar elements for quick access, functioning as a guide, glossary and refresher.
5. Footnotes, if required for specifying relevant details about the sections.

Each section has a structure as well, that being:
1. Title
2. Objective of this section
3. Contents
4. Introduction to the topic discussed, so the flow of information can be smoother.
5. A linear development of ideas.
6. Summary and Conclusions

# On the topic of sources and Wikipedia

The sources of information for this book are, mostly, my understanding of different topics throughout my academic journey, however, if you're reading this guide that means the system might have failed you, as such, one cannot only rely on an academic setting to learn, this then leads to the usage of the biggest source of information nowadays, followed by one closely related result of it, the Internet and Wikipedia. 

Wikipedia itself is not a bad source of information, although, it is, not the best source one could utilize, however, there has been a common misunderstanding of how it works and why it can be unreliable at times, since it's a common, user-driven knowledge base, anyone can add, modify and erase information, be it truth or falsehoods, additionally, moderators constantly check and evaluate changes, so misinformation can be deleted and changed quickly, as such, unless you are particularly lucky in finding a recent change on an article that's misleading, it is seldom one finds any discrepancies with factual data.

This doesn't mean it is a heavenly source of information, specially for research, as it is user-driven, it means the users might not be experts and could ignore citing their sources too, whilst the information is mostly correct, it can also be naive and short, this proves useful when quickly searching for certain results or unstructured learning, but it's detrimental if you need anything more formal and it's specially hurtful when doing any important research.

Even though it is a source I recommend for learning math, the structure and extension of the topics isn't good enough if you want to do a deeper dive into certain fields and topics, however, for most students, one can get really deep into the foundations of math without requiring a career on it, and the information is sufficient for the majority of common people

Finally, whilst recommended for any doubts that may surge if one doesn't understand the full overview of a topic, I cannot fully endorse it as your only source of information, if any question is more profound and remains unanswered after a few minutes of reading some articles, it is advised to do adequate research with better sources, and, to cite and source books for this matter.

Below are both books I read before and during the making of this primer, and also, books I recommend for diving deeper into the more complex fields, as each one can be thoroughly dissected, mathematics is an extremely dense topic one cannot fully know everything about.
- Rosen, K. (2011). *Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications.* McGraw-Hill Education.
- Shafarevich, I. R., & Remizov, A. (2012). *Linear Algebra and Geometry.* Springer.

# Motivations for this primer

After entering high school, and throughout my academic career, I realized my peers had, to say the least, at most, a basic understanding of the simplest level of arithmetic one could get, but it is not their fault of course, since one learns those concepts from school, it is the consequence of a system made for you to do, not to learn, we will introduce a few concepts to understand better why that viewpoint can't align itself with math nicely at first.

A *Pragmatic paradigm* for teaching is what can normally be seen at a basic and intermediate level of academics, straight forward and easy to teach, one only needs to worry about how to do things, not why they work or what they are, we are taught how to do basic arithmetic and then algebra, but we are never given an explanation of how things work, why they are that way and what precisely do they mean, how to interpret them, why exactly they are applied in such a way that provides usefulness.

The problem being, you are taught how to do, not how to think, and math is not just about doing, but first and foremost, before any process is done, one must think what are we trying to represent, what are we dealing with, what is this expression trying to tell me, as such, an idealistic, theoretical and thought focused method aligns nicely with the intricacies that define the intrinsic complexity that lies on the beauty of math.

This *primer*[^2] aims to teach the foundations of mathematics, so we can first think and understand, which is the hardest part of any scientific field, forgetting all mechanized procedures instilled in our brain, and beginning to shape our mind.
